# Narration script: Friction Gap Atlas briefing

The story is told bottom-up: from what friction is, to who needs help, to today's gloves, then the five mechanisms, the gaps, and what the review delivers.

- **Voice:** ElevenLabs "Bella – Professional, Bright, Warm" (premade, American; voice_id `hpp4J3VqNfWAUOO0d1Us`).
- **Model:** eleven_v4.
- **Sources:** every number comes from `review-landscape-visual-data.md` or `draft-abstract-introduction.md`.

| # | Scene | Narration | On-screen picture |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook | [warmly] Picture someone lifting a cup of coffee after a stroke. Their hand closes… but the cup keeps slipping. Today I'll show you a different way to fix that. Not a stronger hand, but a grippier one. | Two finger pads and a cup that slides down; then a "grippier" contact holds it |
| 2 | Friction | Let's start from the bottom: friction. Engineers describe grip with one number, called the friction coefficient, written as the Greek letter mu. A low mu means slippery, like wet glass. A high mu means grippy, like rubber on rubber. | A μ scale with slippery and grippy ends |
| 3 | The lever | Here's why mu matters. To hold a half-kilogram cup between two fingers, the squeeze you need is the weight divided by twice the friction, plus a small safety margin. At a slippery mu of 0.3, each finger must push about 10.6 newtons. Raise mu to 0.8, and that drops to about 4 newtons. That's 62 percent less effort. | Equation; curve; a dot slides from 0.3 to 0.8 |
| 4 | Who needs it | And many people need that help. About 94 million people worldwide are living after a stroke, and 40 to 50 out of every 100 survivors still have a weakened arm and hand. Ageing adds to it: older skin is more slippery, so older adults squeeze about twice as hard to lift the same object. | 100-square grid; 1× vs 2× bars |
| 5 | Today's gloves | Today's main answer is the soft robotic glove. It adds force using actuators: small motors, cables, or air bladders that bend the fingers. These gloves do help patients. But complete systems weigh between 335 and 562 grams, and they depend on pumps and batteries. | Mass scale; heavy force gloves vs light friction wearables |
| 6 | Active methods | So our review asks: what if the glove raised friction instead? We compare five ways. Some are active, meaning they need power to switch on. Suction pulls a small vacuum, like an octopus sucker. Jamming stiffens a soft pad by pulling the air out. And electroadhesion uses a voltage to make the surface cling, like static on a balloon. | Suction cup, jamming pad, electroadhesion charges |
| 7 | Adhesion | Adhesion comes in two forms. Smart adhesives switch from sticky to non-sticky with gentle heat. Dry adhesives copy the gecko's foot: tiny hairs that cling to smooth surfaces with no glue and no power at all. | Thermometer and adhesive pad; gecko-style micro-hairs |
| 8 | Kirigami | The fifth is kirigami, the art of cutting paper. Cut rows of slits into a thin sheet, then stretch it, and the flaps pop up into tiny scales, like snake skin. Those scales change friction by three to more than ten times, with no motor and no battery. It has already been used on shoe soles to prevent slips on ice. | A slit sheet stretches and pops into scales; μ ×3 → ×10 |
| 9 | Where the research is | But here's the catch. Most of this work was done on robots. In our search, each idea took three to nine years to reach a human hand. And kirigami hasn't reached the hand at all, even though bending a finger naturally stretches the skin, which is exactly the motion kirigami needs. | Robot vs hand timeline lanes; kirigami lane empty |
| 10 | Measurement | The second problem is measurement. Studies report gains from 1.4 times to 2,000 times. But they measure different things. Some measure friction, some measure how well a surface switches on and off, and others measure stiffness. It's like comparing kilometres with kilograms. So no one can rank these methods for a glove yet. | Log-scale dots grouped by what they measured |
| 11 | Trade-off | In our preliminary scoring, a pattern appears. The methods you can switch on demand bring back pumps, heaters or high voltage, the very bulk we want to remove. The light, power-free corner holds only dry adhesion and kirigami. | Burden vs control map; target box |
| 12 | The review | So our review does three things. It puts all five methods on one map, sorted by the physics of how they grip. It compares them with shared measures, like friction gained per gram added. And it sets out a roadmap and a standard test, with kirigami skins as a new direction for the hand. | Gap × contribution grid fills |
| 13 | Close | The big idea is simple. Helping a weak hand doesn't have to mean bigger motors. If we engineer friction, a light glove can hold more, and that's the path to grasp assistance people will actually wear. | Back to the cup, now held |
