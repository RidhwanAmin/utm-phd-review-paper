Friction Gap Atlas

Assistive gloves mostly try to squeeze harder. A glove can also stop objects slipping by raising friction at the contact. This atlas maps what is known about five ways of doing that (suction, jamming, adhesion, electroadhesion and kirigami), where the evidence runs out, and what the review will deliver.

Part 1 Context

Grasping fails when friction runs short

Hand impairment is common, and a weak or ageing hand loses grip partly because contact friction becomes less reliable. Raising friction directly reduces the force a glove has to supply.

Part 2 Current work

Gloves add force and mass, and friction research stays on robots

Force-actuated gloves work in clinical tests, but complete systems are heavy. The friction mechanisms that could lighten them are developed mostly on robot grippers and crawlers, and reach the hand years later or not at all.

Part 3 What is lacking

No common yardstick, and the controllable options carry the hardware

Each mechanism reports its success in a different unit, so they cannot be ranked for a glove. When they are scored against what a wearer needs, the mechanisms that switch on demand are the ones that bring back pumps, heaters and high voltage.

Part 4 How the review closes it

Each gap gets a primary answer from at least one part of the review

What the review will add

  1. A shared map. A passive/active taxonomy organised by contact physics (interlocking, van der Waals, pressure difference, electrostatic, stiffness), replacing five separate literatures.
  2. A shared yardstick. Friction gain Δμ, switching ratio and time, gain per added gram and per watt, and voltage, so the numbers in Fig 6 sit on one axis.
  3. An honest trade-off. When on-demand control is worth its pumps, heaters or high voltage, and when a passive surface is enough (Fig 7b).
  4. A new direction. Strain-triggered kirigami skins, switched on by the wearer's own finger flexion, as power-free friction modulation for the hand: the empty cells in Fig 4 and Fig 5.
  5. A test protocol. Standard benchmarks plus user outcomes (touch, comfort, safety) that today's studies rarely report.
